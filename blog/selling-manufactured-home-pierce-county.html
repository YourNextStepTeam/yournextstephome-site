Selling a Manufactured Home in Pierce County: The Title Nightmare That Almost Killed This Deal Three Times

August 12, 2026 · 7 min read · By Becca Pitts, eXp Realty, Pierce County WA

Photo placeholder: Exterior shot of the manufactured home on its rural Pierce County lot, showing the property from the road with surrounding trees and gravel driveway

Here's something most people don't know about manufactured homes in Washington State: they have two titles. One is recorded with the county, like any other piece of real estate. The other lives with the Department of Licensing, like a vehicle. And when those two titles don't match? You've got a problem that can kill a deal before it even gets started.

I learned this in vivid detail on a deal in rural Pierce County that almost fell apart three separate times before it finally closed. This is that story. Honestly, it's the kind of transaction that makes me love this work even on the days it's making me question my life choices.

What Happens When Your Manufactured Home Title Doesn't Match?

A couple contacted me about selling their manufactured home on a few acres out in rural Pierce County. They'd owned the property for years, paid their mortgage on time, kept the place in good shape. On paper, it should have been a straightforward listing. A home on land, a reasonable asking price, and motivated sellers who were ready to move on. Simple, right?

Not even close.

When I started pulling title work, the first red flag showed up almost immediately. The Department of Licensing records listed a different owner than what the county had on file. Not the sellers. Not even the people they'd bought from. The DOL records pointed to someone two owners back, a person the sellers had never even heard of.

In Washington, manufactured homes that haven't gone through the "title elimination" process still carry that DOL title, which is completely separate from the deed to the land. If those records don't align, no title company is going to insure the sale. And if no title company will insure it, no buyer can get a loan. And if no buyer can get a loan, you're stuck with a home you can't sell and a problem you didn't create.

So that was day one. I called the title company, explained the situation, and got the response I was half expecting: "We can't touch this."

I thanked them, hung up, and started making a list. Because when the standard path doesn't work, you build a new one.

Why Do So Many Manufactured Home Sales Fall Apart in Pierce County?

Pierce County has a lot of manufactured homes, especially once you get out past Spanaway, into Graham, Eatonville, and the more rural pockets east of Puyallup. Many of these homes were placed on private land decades ago, and the paperwork trail from that era can be (to put it politely) inconsistent.

Here's what typically goes wrong:

The DOL title was never eliminated

When a manufactured home is permanently affixed to land and the owner wants to treat it as real property instead of personal property, they need to go through a title elimination process with the DOL. A lot of people skip this step, either because they don't know about it or because their original agent didn't mention it. That creates a split in the record that surfaces years later, usually at the worst possible moment.

Liens follow the DOL title, not the deed

This was the second gut punch on my deal. Not only was the DOL title in someone else's name, but that someone else had liens attached to it. A previous owner, two sales back, had unpaid debts that were recorded against the manufactured home's DOL title. Those liens were still active. The sellers had no idea, and honestly, there's no reason they would have known. Nobody told them when they bought the property, and nothing in the county records would have flagged it.

Title companies run from complexity

I contacted three title companies before I found one willing to even look at this file. Most title officers see a DOL mismatch with active liens and politely decline. They're not being difficult; they're protecting their company from liability. But that doesn't help my clients, who just want to sell their home and move on with their lives.

Also, this is the part of the story where I should mention that three other agents had apparently looked at this listing before the sellers called me. All three passed. I don't blame them (manufactured home title issues are genuinely time-consuming), but I also wasn't going to be agent number four to walk away.

How Did I Untangle a Title That Three Other Agents Walked Away From?

Okay, so here's the part where things got interesting. And by "interesting," I mean time-consuming, frustrating, and occasionally absurd.

Step one: the DOL office

I drove 45 miles each way to the Department of Licensing office to pull the original manufactured home title records in person. Yes, in person. Some of this paperwork isn't available online, and when it is, the records are often incomplete or outdated. I needed to see the actual chain of title on the DOL side, and the only way to do that was to show up, take a number, and wait.

What I found confirmed what the title company had flagged. The DOL title had been transferred once, then apparently abandoned in the system when the property sold a second time. Nobody filed the right paperwork to move the DOL title to the current owners. It just sat there, frozen, still showing the name of someone who hadn't owned the home in over a decade.

Step two: the liens

Those liens from the previous owner? They weren't going to clear themselves. I spent the better part of two days on the phone tracking down the creditor, which had since been acquired by another company, which had since merged with a third company. Every call started with me explaining the situation from scratch to a new person who had never heard of the original debtor or the original lien.

Eventually, I reached someone who could confirm that the debt had actually been paid years ago, but the lien release was never recorded. They agreed to issue a new release, but it took three weeks and four follow-up calls before the paperwork actually arrived. I'm not exaggerating when I say I had that creditor's customer service number memorized by the end.

Step three: convincing the title company

Even with the lien release in hand and the DOL records corrected, the title company was cautious. Understandably so. I put together a complete package for them: the corrected DOL records, the lien release, a chain of title summary I'd built from the county and DOL records, and a cover letter explaining the full history of the property's ownership. The underwriter reviewed it and, after what felt like a small eternity, agreed to insure the sale.

Three near-death moments

The deal almost died three times during this process. Once when the first title company declined outright. Once when the creditor's records initially showed the lien as still active (before I tracked down the corrected release). And once when the buyer's lender got nervous about the manufactured home classification and requested additional documentation that took another week to compile.

But it closed. It actually closed. The sellers called me the day after closing, and I could hear the relief in their voices. They'd spent months thinking their home was unsellable. Turns out it wasn't unsellable. It just needed someone stubborn enough to sort through the paperwork.

What Should You Know Before Selling a Manufactured Home in Washington?

If you own a manufactured home in Pierce County (or anywhere in Washington) and you're thinking about selling, here's what I'd tell you over coffee before I even mention listing price.

Check your DOL title status now, not later

Go to the Department of Licensing and find out if your manufactured home still has an active DOL title. If it does, and it doesn't match your deed, you'll want to sort that out before you list. This process can take weeks or even months, and you don't want to be doing it under the pressure of a pending sale with a buyer who's getting impatient and a closing date that keeps sliding.

Ask about title elimination

If your manufactured home is permanently affixed to your land (on a permanent foundation, connected to utilities, not going anywhere), you may be eligible to eliminate the DOL title entirely. This converts the home from personal property to real property, which simplifies the sale dramatically. It also typically makes the home eligible for conventional financing, which opens up your buyer pool significantly. More qualified buyers means better offers.

Pull a preliminary title report early

Before you list, have a title company run a preliminary report. This is where old liens, recording errors, and ownership gaps show up. It's much better to discover these issues when you have time to fix them than to find out two weeks before closing when everyone's stressed and the buyer is threatening to walk. I've started doing this as a standard part of my listing process for manufactured homes, because surprises at closing help nobody.

Work with an agent who won't run from the paperwork

I'm not going to pretend this is glamorous work. Driving 90 miles round trip to the DOL, calling creditors who've been acquired three times over, assembling documentation packages for cautious underwriters: none of that is what you see in the real estate commercials. But it's the work that actually gets manufactured homes sold in Pierce County. If your agent hasn't dealt with DOL title issues, manufactured home lien problems, and reluctant title companies before, you're going to have a rough time.

Every manufactured home sale in Pierce County has its own story. The question isn't whether there will be complications. The question is whether your agent is going to stick around when those complications show up.

The bottom line

Selling a manufactured home in Pierce County isn't always complicated. Plenty of them close without a hitch. But when it is complicated, the problems tend to be the kind that make other agents disappear. Title mismatches, old liens, DOL records from a decade ago, title companies that won't return your calls. I've seen all of it, and I've closed deals that other people said couldn't close.
