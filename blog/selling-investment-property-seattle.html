Selling an Investment Property in Seattle: What a Year-Long Listing in Delridge Taught Me

July 22, 2026 · 7 min read · By Becca Pitts, eXp Realty, Pierce County WA

Photo placeholder: Street-level view of the Delridge neighborhood investment property, showing the home's exterior and the West Seattle streetscape

Some listings close in two weeks. You price it right, stage it well, and the offers roll in before you've even had time to update the photos on the MLS. This was not one of those listings.

This is the story of an investment property in Seattle's Delridge neighborhood that took almost a year to sell. It involved tenant complications, deferred maintenance, a co-listing arrangement with another agent, and more patience than I thought I had in me. Honestly, it was the kind of deal that doesn't make for a glamorous Instagram post, but it taught me more about real estate than a dozen quick closes ever could.

If you're thinking about selling investment property in Seattle (or anywhere in the Puget Sound region), this one's for you.

What Makes Investment Properties in Seattle So Complicated to Sell?

Selling a primary residence and selling an investment property are two completely different animals. When you sell your own home, you clean it up, move your stuff out, and hand over the keys. Simple enough.

Investment properties? Not so much.

First, there are tenants. If someone is living in the property, you can't just list it and start hosting open houses whenever you feel like it. You've got to coordinate showings around their schedule, which means working with people who may or may not be thrilled about the idea of their home being sold. Washington state has specific tenant protections that dictate how much notice you need to give, what access you're allowed, and what happens to the lease when the property changes hands. You don't get to skip any of that.

Then there's the condition of the property. A lot of investment properties have years of deferred maintenance, because the math on rental income doesn't always leave room for a new roof or updated electrical panels. Buyers see those issues during inspections, and suddenly your listing price looks optimistic.

And here's the part that catches a lot of sellers off guard: the buyer pool for investment properties is smaller than you'd expect. You're not marketing to first-time homebuyers who fell in love with the kitchen tile. You're marketing to investors who are running numbers on cap rates, cash-on-cash returns, and rent rolls. These buyers are analytical. They're patient. And they will wait for the price to come to them.

In Seattle specifically, the investment property market has its own rhythm. The city has been through several cycles of rapid appreciation followed by correction, and the rental market brings its own set of regulations that can make investors cautious. Neighborhoods like Delridge, which sits in West Seattle, have seen real changes over the past decade. There's new development, infrastructure improvements, and a growing sense of community identity. But pricing an investment property there still requires understanding the gap between what a neighborhood is becoming and what it is right now.

Why Did This Listing in West Seattle Take Almost a Year?

So here's where the story gets specific.

I co-listed this property with another agent. The home was in Seattle's Delridge neighborhood, a part of West Seattle that's been evolving steadily over the past several years. It was an investment property with a tenant in place, deferred maintenance that needed addressing, and a seller who (understandably) wanted to get a strong return on years of ownership.

The first challenge was pricing. In a shifting market, pricing an investment property is more art than science. You're balancing comparable sales against current rental income, property condition, and buyer expectations. I set the initial price where the market data supported it, but the market had opinions of its own.

The second challenge was the tenant situation. I'm not going to get into specifics here (privacy matters), but coordinating showings and navigating the relationship between seller, tenant, and potential buyers required constant communication. Every showing had to be scheduled carefully. Every piece of feedback had to be filtered through the reality that this was someone's home, not just a line item on a spreadsheet.

The third challenge was the condition of the property itself. Deferred maintenance is one of those things that looks manageable on paper but becomes a negotiation point in every single offer. Buyers would tour the property, express interest, and then come back with repair requests that changed the math on the deal entirely.

And the fourth challenge? Honestly, it was just the market. Seattle's real estate market went through some real turbulence during the listing period. Interest rates were doing their thing. Buyer confidence fluctuated week to week. The pool of investors willing to take on a project property in Delridge was smaller than it would have been two years earlier.

So I adjusted. I revisited the pricing strategy with my co-listing partner. I worked with the seller on addressing some of the maintenance concerns that kept coming up in buyer feedback. I kept communicating with every agent who showed interest, following up on feedback, and keeping the listing visible in the MLS. It wasn't glamorous work. It was the kind of grinding, persistent, "keep showing up" real estate that doesn't get talked about enough.

After approximately a year on the market, the property closed.

How Does Co-Listing With Another Agent Actually Work?

This is a question I get more often than you'd think. Most people assume that every listing has one agent, period. But co-listing (where two agents share the listing responsibilities) is more common than it seems, especially on complicated properties.

Here's how it worked on this one. My co-listing partner and I divided responsibilities based on individual strengths and availability. One agent might handle a round of showings while the other worked on pricing strategy or coordinated with the seller on repair decisions. Communication between my co-listing partner and me had to be airtight, because nothing derails a listing faster than conflicting information going to the seller or to potential buyers.

The benefit of co-listing is straightforward: two networks, two sets of problem-solving skills, and two agents who can keep the energy going on a difficult listing. On a listing that drags out for months, having a partner means you can stay fresh. When one agent is feeling burned out or frustrated (and believe me, that happens on a year-long listing), the other can step in and bring renewed focus.

The challenge is also straightforward: you've got to be on the same page. Every pricing decision, every counter-offer, every conversation with the seller has to be coordinated. If you're not communicating well, a co-listing can create more problems than it solves.

On this listing, the partnership worked. Were there moments where my co-listing partner and I had different opinions on strategy? Absolutely. But I talked through those differences openly, and my co-listing partner and I made decisions together and presented a united front to the seller and to the market. I'd co-list again with the right partner on the right property, without hesitation.

"The easy listings teach you that your pricing strategy works. The hard ones teach you who you are as an agent."

What I Learned From the Longest Listing of My Career

Okay, so what's the takeaway? What do you actually learn from a listing that takes almost a year to close?

Patience is a skill, not a personality trait. I'm naturally someone who likes to move fast, get things done, and cross items off the list. This listing taught me that some deals just take time. You can do everything right (price it based on solid data, market it aggressively, communicate constantly) and still be waiting months for the right buyer to show up. That doesn't mean you're failing. It means the market is moving at its own pace, and your job is to stay ready.

Communication is the thing that holds everything together. When a listing drags on, the seller starts to wonder what's happening. Are you still working on it? Is anyone looking? What's the plan? I learned to over-communicate. Weekly updates, even when the update was "nothing new this week, but here's what I'm doing to keep the listing visible." Silence is the worst thing you can give a seller on a long listing.

Deferred maintenance is a bigger deal than most sellers realize. If you're thinking about selling an investment property in Seattle (or anywhere), start addressing the obvious stuff before you list. A fresh coat of paint, basic landscaping, fixing that one faucet that's been dripping for three years. These things cost a fraction of what they'll cost you in negotiation concessions later. I've seen $500 worth of repairs prevent $5,000 in buyer credits.

Co-listing can be a genuine advantage, but only with the right partner. It's not about splitting the work in half. It's about doubling the effort and bringing complementary skills to a situation that one person might not be able to handle alone. The key is communication, clear roles, and mutual respect for each other's judgment.

Every listing teaches you something. This is the big one for me. The quick, clean closings teach you that your systems work. The hard ones teach you who you are when things get complicated. This listing in Delridge taught me that I can stick with a difficult deal, adapt when the market shifts underneath me, and get it across the finish line even when it takes way longer than anyone expected. That's not something you learn from a textbook or a weekend seminar. You learn it by living through it.

Thinking About Selling an Investment Property in Seattle?

If you're sitting on an investment property in Seattle, West Seattle, Delridge, or anywhere in the Puget Sound region, and you're thinking about selling, here's my honest advice.

Start with a realistic conversation about the property's condition, the tenant situation (if there is one), and what the current market is actually doing. Not what it was doing six months ago. What it's doing right now, today, in your specific neighborhood.

Also, don't be afraid of a listing that takes time. Not every property sells in the first weekend, and that's okay. What matters is having an agent who will stick with it, adjust the strategy when needed, and keep you informed every step of the way.

I do most of my work in Pierce County (Tacoma, Bonney Lake, Puyallup, and the surrounding areas), but this listing in Seattle reminded me that the principles of good real estate are the same everywhere: know the property, know the market, and don't quit on your client.
